A very abbreviated biography

The long way
to the fruit bowl.

  1. c. 8,000 BCE

    Wild beginnings

    In New Guinea and Southeast Asia, people begin selecting wild Musa plants. The ancestors of today’s bananas are small and packed with hard seeds.

  2. Over millennia

    A seedless success

    Farmers favor rare plants with fleshy, nearly seedless fruit and propagate them by shoots. Trade carries different banana groups across Asia, Africa, and the Pacific.

  3. 1500s–1800s

    Across an ocean

    Bananas spread through the tropical Americas. Better ships and refrigeration later turn a fragile local fruit into a major export crop.

  4. Early 1900s

    Big Mike rules

    Gros Michel—nicknamed “Big Mike”—becomes the dominant export banana: sturdy, sweet, and well suited to shipping.

  5. 1950s–today

    The Cavendish era

    Panama disease devastates Gros Michel plantations. Exporters switch to resistant Cavendish varieties, which now fill most Western supermarkets.

The question everyone asks

Why does banana candy taste nothing like a banana?

One note, turned up loud

Many classic banana sweets lean heavily on isoamyl acetate, a fruity-smelling ester also found in real bananas. Isolated and concentrated, it reads as bright, sweet, and unmistakably “candy.”

An entire flavor orchestra

A real banana’s aroma comes from a mixture of many volatile compounds. Add changing ripeness, mild acidity, sugar, and creamy texture, and the result is rounder and much more complex.

Short answer Candy doesn’t copy the whole fruit. It spotlights one recognizable part of the fruit’s aroma.

What about the “lost banana” story?

Banana candy was not bottled from Gros Michel.

You may have heard that artificial banana flavor perfectly matches the Gros Michel banana, while today’s Cavendish does not. It is a great story—but too neat.

Synthetic fruity esters were being made in the nineteenth century, before bananas were common in many American markets. Gros Michel may strike some tasters as more candy-like, but both Gros Michel and Cavendish contain isoamyl acetate, alongside many other aroma compounds. The candy flavor was not a chemical snapshot of one lost variety.

And Gros Michel isn’t extinct. It is still grown in some places; it simply lost its starring role in global exports.

The peel-away

“Artificial” banana is not fake so much as simplified.

One loud molecule became a flavor of its own—while the living fruit kept the full recipe.
